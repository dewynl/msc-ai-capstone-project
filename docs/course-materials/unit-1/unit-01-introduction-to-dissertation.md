# Unit 1: An Introduction to Your Dissertation

# **What is a dissertation?**

## **Project Planning**

As this is going to be your project you will have a responsibility to define a project plan to help you manage and deliver the associated work. This is a valuable skill that can be applied across a range of activities and is essential when delivering such a large piece of work.

## **Time Management**

The notion of time management goes hand in hand with the project planning aspects of a dissertation. A project such as this needs to have time properly allocated to it, this is not something that you can achieve in 1-2 weeks of hard work! Spending time making a plan and managing your time might seem like a waste of valuable research and development time but in reality, you will definitely see the benefits in the long term

## **Analysis and Evaluation**

A dissertation will, almost without exception, require a wide range of data to be analysed and evaluated in order to make decisions and reach conclusions. This can be in the initial stages when reviewing the existing literature around a topic to understand the current state of the art and determining the niche filled by your project or it can be the analysis of the data that you have collected as part of your development or experimentation

## **Communication Skills**

Communication is a huge part of any dissertation whether that is in the final written report or in an oral presentation. The way you explain your research and your dissertation is very important and so one of the key goals of the dissertation is to develop your own written and oral communication skills. After all if you can’t explain your work then how will anyone ever benefit from it?

## **Situating Your Work in a Wider Context**

Finally, we have already discussed the importance of understanding the work that has gone before and how you might fit into the wider world, and then building on that appropriately.

# Research

## Types of Research

**There are 2 types of research that you may want to consider for your project:**

1. Primary.
2. Secondary.

However, in reality, your dissertation will usually end up using both approaches throughout the course of the dissertation.

### Primary Research

Primary research aims to collect original data for a specific research problem and then analyze and report the findings.

Primary research will involve creating data, whether this is the output of an experiment, a piece of software or survey results. One of the considerations when carrying out primary research is the need to obtain ethical approval, which although potentially time-consuming is utterly essential. We will discuss ethical considerations in a future lecturecast.

### Secondary Research

The second type of research that you can carry out is secondary research, which typically relies on secondary sources for your data

This includes an in-depth review of existing literature and critical analysis of existing data, which in reality can be collected by anyone. When carrying out secondary research it is important to consider the methods that have been used, the conclusions that have been and any limitations on the work.

It’s entirely likely that you will employ both secondary and primary research on your dissertation. For example, your literature review will be in the form of secondary research but your development or experimentation will be primary research.

## **Carving a Research Space (CARS)**

Once you have narrowed down your focus then it is important to carve your specific niche within the space you have identified.

We are going to look at something called Swale’s CARS model, which is designed to help you find your niche in the area.

**The model uses 3 different approaches for generating your research space.**

1. **Counter-Claiming:** First of all, we have counter-claiming, where you are challenging earlier research. For example, you might find that Smith et al. believe method X is accurate but your examination indicates this might have weaknesses.
2. **Indicating a Gap**: The second is indicating where there’s a gap, you highlight that this earlier work does not adequately address all of the questions or problems. For example, while existing studies have established X they have not addressed Y or Z.
3. Question-Raising: The final is question raising, where you will ask questions about earlier work, and suggest how further work could address these issues. For example, if Smith et al. have established something but it raises more questions than it answered.

## Research Question

One of the core elements of a dissertation is what you are actually going to focus your work on.

A research question provides you with an overarching question that you are aiming to answer with the work undertaken in your dissertation. It is one of the key elements of a dissertation and it is central to the success of your project. It provides a way of pinpointing exactly what you want to find out and if you can define a clear and concise question then it will give your work focus and purpose.

**Broadly speaking a research question should satisfy the following 6 criteria:**

1. **Focused:** It should be focused, meaning that you are aiming to answer a single problem or issue. It is very easy to let the scope of your project expand to an unmanageable level and so a well-targeted research question will help you pinpoint your niche.
2. **Researchable**: It should be researchable in that it is something you can actually achieve using either primary or secondary research methods.
3. **Feasible**: Your dissertation is meant to highlight your knowledge and experiences to date; however, it also needs to be something that you can deliver in the allotted time. A very common issue with a dissertation project is that a student will aim to achieve too much! Ensuring your project is manageable and feasible with the current timeline is essential when defining and scoping your research question.
4. **Specific**: It might be tempting to give yourself a very open-ended question to keep your options open, however, ensuring that you make your question targeted and specific gives you the opportunity to answer it well and properly highlight your strengths. It is often better to answer a very specific bounded question well than to try and tackle a broad, general question.
5. **Complex:** However, despite the need for focus and specificity your dissertation is still a substantial piece of work and as such you need to develop a question that is complex enough for you to fully demonstrate your capabilities as an independent researcher.
6. **Relevant:** Finally, it is important to make your research question relevant. This might be ensuring you are relevant to your field of study, or that it is relevant to the current landscape of research in your area. For example, a computing project looking at the importance of Ada as a programming language could be considered slightly irrelevant now!

### Types of Research Questions

Whether you are going to undertake qualitative or quantitative research you are going to use a research question. The kind of question that you are going to use will depend on the kind of research that you want to do and what it is you are trying to find out.

Broadly speaking there are a number of different categories of research questions.

1. **Descriptive:** Descriptive questions are actively seeking to describe a situation, population or phenomenon, for example what are the characteristics of a good authentication system?
2. **Comparative:** Comparative questions look to understand the differences between things that you are investigating, for example, what are the differences between biometric and traditional identification systems?
3. **Correlational:** Correlational questions aim to find links between different variables. This could be a positive correlation, for example, the relationship between the size of a file and the time it takes to download, or a negative correlation, for instance, as your bandwidth increases the time to download decreases and finally no correlation at all. For example, your bandwidth has no bearing on how many words per minute you can type. An example of a correlational question might include, does a person’s age determine the amount of time they spend on social media?
4. **Exploratory:** Exploratory questions are designed to improve the understanding a particular phenomenon, for example, what factors will impact a user’s ability to recall passwords?
5. **Evaluation:** Evaluation research aims to better understand the strengths and weaknesses or performance of something, for example, what are the advantages and disadvantages of multifactor authentication?
6. **Action:** Action research looks to understand how something may be achieved or improved, for example, what is the most effective strategy for improving the memorability of passwords.

# Title, Aims and Objectives

## Title

The title of your dissertation is an important choice, it’s the thing that will provide a first impression about your work and help readers to understand what you are focusing on.

At this stage we want to consider a working title for the project, but it is more than likely something that will evolve over the course of the project. A good title will help to provide an insight into the topic that you are going to cover and illustrate the type of study that you are going to carry out.

Your title should be clear and informative, giving any reader what they need to know as soon as they look at your work. It’s important to keep in mind that the most important thing about the title of your dissertation is that it should clearly communicate what you are writing about, which can be broken down into a topic and a focus. The topic of your research is the general area and the focus is what you’re investigating in the field. For example, in a dissertation titled ‘Understanding the effects of social media: An investigation of privacy perceptions’ then the topic the topic is social media usage and the focus is user perceptions of privacy.

The research approach or method that you use might have a major impact on the design and results of your study and therefore it might be relevant to consider amending the title to consider the approach used. In our previous example of a dissertation looking at the privacy perceptions of social media, we could modify this to use a specific case study and amend our title to be ‘Understanding the effects of social media: An qualitative analysis of privacy perceptions’.

While it might be tempting to have a lengthy and descriptive title it is always best to err on the side of clarity, as you really don’t want your readers to be confused from the outset!

## Aims and Objectives

Up to now we’ve talked about developing a research problem and a research question, as these are the two things that start you on your journey to defining and proposing your dissertation.

Now we are going to move on to look at the aims and objectives of your research, which you are going to develop to help really focus your research and the development of your dissertation.

Aims and objectives are often used interchangeably but they are very different things that support and guide your research in very different ways.

### Aims

**The aims of your dissertation are the things that you want to achieve.**

Typically, your dissertation or any research project will have a single overarching aim that defines what you are going to achieve.

You can think of an aim as a statement of intent, and they are usually written in broad terms to define what you want to have done by the end of your dissertation.

**Let’s look at some examples of possible aims for computing-related projects.**

To investigate the relationship between social media usage and demographics, such as, age, gender and education.

Here we have expressed a broad overarching statement of what we want to achieve. Notice that there is no mention of how we will achieve the aim at this point, it is just a very high-level statement.

To determine the best machine learning-based approach to provide movie recommendations based on viewing habits.

This aim wants to determine the best machine learning approach to provide recommendations based on previous behaviours, again we don’t have a defined approach at this stage.

To understand the impact of encryption algorithms on the performance of a mobile device.

The last aim is to understand the impact of encryption on the performance of mobile devices

Whereas an aim is a very high-level statement of what you want to achieve, objectives are specific statements that define measurable outcomes; in essence, they are the steps or actions that you will take to support the delivery of your aim.

It is always a good idea to use strong positive verbs when you are constructing objectives such as collect, construct, classify, develop.

**You may have heard people mention SMART objectives previously, and that is something that we want to apply to the objectives for your dissertation.**

- Specific: Specific means that your objectives should be precise and clear about what you are going to do.
- Measurable: Measurable means that you will be able to quantify when you have finished your objective.
- Achievable: Achievable means that you should ensure what you want to do is something that you can manage. It is often more important to have a less ambitious objective that you can complete rather than something that you will never deliver.
- **Realistic:** Realistic considers whether you have the necessary resources, skills, or time to deliver your objective.
- **Time Constrained**: Finally, time constrained or timely means that you have a timeline and end date for your objective.

### **Example Objectives**

If we take our first aim, we looked at *'To investigate the relationship between social media usage and demographics, such as age, gender, and education,'* then we might consider the following objectives:

- Develop appropriate web scraping methods using the Twitter API.
- Design a survey to capture user perceptions of social media usage.
- Create a visual dashboard to present the results.

## Writing a Proposal

The next important thing to consider is distilling all of your research, thoughts and ideas into a research proposal.

This is a more detailed description of your project and the research that you have outlined so far.

The research proposal will build on the thinking that you have done so far in defining the research problem, any discussions that you have had with your supervisor and your initial research and reading. A research proposal will help you to reflect on what you are going to do and will help you when you come to the writing up stage of your project. Your proposal should tie together a lot of the elements that we discussed so far to form a coherent and structured document to develop your project going forwards.

So far we have talked about the key elements when considering your dissertation in terms of the research problem that you have identified, the gap in the current research and the question that you want to answer. We’ve also talked about outlining your aim and objectives.

The remainder of this lecturecast is going to look at how you might start to tie this all together into the outline of a research proposal.

### Why is this important?

A research proposal is a really important document as it helps you to begin to shape the idea of your dissertation into something tangible, which you can begin to craft into a something engaging and interesting.

It essentially offers a link between where you currently are with your research and thinking and provides a means to link this to the endpoint and goal of your research.

Developing your proposal helps you to refine your thinking and iteratively hone in on your chosen project, research questions and research design. This is something that is usually used to help you identify an appropriate supervisor and then with their help, shape the proposal accordingly.

The approach to writing your proposal is essentially an iterative process where you will understand the area and space you are interested in, using the CARS-type approach discussed previously to identify your research problem and the subsequent question.

This question is then further refined as you review the literature and gather new understanding and insight, which you can use to drive the development of your research approach. We will discuss research methods more in a future lecturecast.

# **Purpose of a Literature Review**

There are several reasons as to why it is important to carry out a thorough literature review, beyond the fact that the mark scheme says you have to!

First of all, it helps to provide some justification for your research question and can support your core arguments.

At this point, you want to understand what you are aiming to do, why you want to do it and what is already known.

You will use the literature to answer the question of what has been done to date in your research area.

Increased awareness and understanding of what has been done to date will allow you to contextualise and outline your research topic, which will quantify the relevance of your current research.

Performing the literature review will give you a deeper understanding of the subject as you develop your review and prepare your report. When you begin a literature review you are looking at descriptions of the work, interpreting and analysing the research.

**This information and understanding are used to synthesise your literature review.**

Finally, the literature review is a really useful document throughout the course of your dissertation as it provides a benchmark for you to compare your findings as you go.

The literature review should be something that you develop early on in the proposal stage, which you will return to and update throughout the course of the project as you learn more about your subject and as new research becomes available.

# Lifecycle of a Literature Review

As previously mentioned, the literature review process is something that will run alongside the entirety of your dissertation and can be broken down into three stages.

1. First, you will perform an early review to help you identify your research topic and to develop your project proposal. At this point, you are performing a high-level review of the subject area to locate your position in the topic and carve out a niche. This stage is all about understanding what has been done before and its relevance.
2. Next, you will perform the literature review itself, where you are taking a deeper dive into the related work to fully understand the strengths, weaknesses, and gaps in the subject area. This is where you will look to demonstrate your critical analysis skills as you evaluate the research. It should be noted that this is not something that is done once and then discarded; you will need to regularly keep abreast of the latest research and developments in the area to update your review appropriately.
3. Finally, you will take your literature review and bring it into your dissertation.

# Types of Review

Paré et al (2015) define several different types of literature review, each of which aims to present the information in a different way, following a slightly different process.

## Narrative

A narrative review will take an opportunistic look at the literature and evidence that is readily available, with very little explanation of the strategy. In its simplest form, the narrative review attempts to identify what has been written on a subject or topic.

## Descriptive

Descriptive reviews seek to determine the extent to which a body of empirical studies in a specific research area supports or reveals any interpretable patterns or trends with respect to pre-existing propositions, theories, methodologies or findings.

## Scoping

A scoping review begins with an initial indication of the size and nature of the available literature with inclusion and exclusion search criteria being used. This is very much a blue sky approach to a review.

## Theoretical

A theoretical review searches for existing theories and empirical research to develop, extend or refine a model or to devise a hypothesis.

## Systematic

A systematic review uses specific search and data collation strategies to perform a structured and exhaustive review.

## Critical

A critical review will analyse the literature to highlight any weaknesses, contradictions, or inconsistencies.

We've talked a lot about the strategies and aims of a literature review, but what do we actually mean when we talk about literature?

The answer is that pretty much anything can be considered as literature, but not all literature is created equal. Above the line, we have peer-reviewed sources of literature, meaning that they have been independently reviewed and validated for rigour and originality. This type of peer-reviewed literature would typically be considered a gold standard because of the rigour applied to the review processes.

However, that's not to say that there isn't a place for other types of literature, such as those shown below the line. Everything from a whitepaper to a newspaper article can be thought of as literature and does have a place in your research. This is particularly relevant for your projects, which are likely to consider technology. This is an area that moves so quickly that sometimes journals and textbooks just cannot keep up with the rapid changes. In this instance, blogs, whitepapers, news articles, and the like are going to be very helpful.

While there is no right answer for where your literature should come from, a good rule of thumb is to think in terms of 60/40, with at least 60% drawn from peer-reviewed sources and the remaining 40% from other sources.

# Written Documents

The majority of the available sources will make use of will be written documents, and so it is important to consider the advantages and disadvantages associated with them.

## Advantages

In terms of advantages, a written document is unobtrusive and easy to manage, offering a low-cost means of gathering data. The ubiquity of the internet means that there is an easily available source of written data, which, for the most part, is easily available, providing an auditable trail of evidence. This also provides a source that is easy to critically evaluate.

## Disadvantages

There is certainly potential for personal motivations or bias to be introduced with written documents, and so remaining objective and inquisitive is imperative. While the internet certainly makes data more readily available, sometimes academic articles particularly can be difficult to locate because of paywalls and restrictions.

# Evaluating a Source

When evaluating a source, there are five criteria that you should take into account:

## Relevance:

First of all how relevant is the source that you have collected, and does it meet your intended research focus? If so, then you will need to understand the level of coverage provided by the source. For example, it might provide a very basic overview or in-depth coverage. You will also need to consider how the source can be classified, whether that is theory, evidence, police,y or conjecture.

## Objectivity

Next, you should consider the objectivity of the source. It is possible that there could be an agenda behind the work so it is important to understand the author and their credibility or neutrality. We have previously talked about peer-reviewed sources so something that can be considered is whether the source has been subject to a review process.

## Accuracy

The accuracy of the source is important to consider, as you need to understand what theory, facts or evidence are being presented. This can be supported if you can find other sources to verify the information being presented.

## Timeliness

The state of the art changes very quickly, particularly in a subject such as computing or cybersecurity and so it is important to consider when the source was written to ascertain whether it contains up-to-date information and whether it is still relevant.

## Usability

Finally, it is important to consider the usability of the source, based on how it will fit into your overall narrative of your research.

# Literature Review Tips

### Don't leave until the last minute!

The literature review is something that needs sufficient time in order to be completed properly and to ensure the best possible result for your project. Therefore, it is important to make sure that you give enough time to the process.

### **Be optimistic (initially).**

While it might seem a substantial task, it is important to be optimistic when starting out the process, and to aim to cover the subject in a comprehensive and systematic way. This will ensure that you have a strong understanding of the literature, which in turn builds a strong foundation for your work.

### Identifying targes

Having a strategy from the outset will be vital to ensure you focus on the correct sources, and so before you start, you should endeavour to identify appropriate targets to achieve as well as the appropriate sources to draw on.

## **Record/audit search strategy.**

### What type of literature is available/informative/authoritative?

Ensure that you keep a record of your strategy so that you can perform your review in a systematic manner to ensure you get the maximum value.

## **Consider the narrative.**

What you are essentially doing with a literature review is telling a story for your reader, to explain the history and location of your research. You are building a narrative of the state of the art and how you have identified a niche within that body of work. Therefore, you need to consider the overall narrative of the review and how you build that accordingly.

# Writing a Literature Review

Let's take a look at some of the skills that might come in handy for writing a literature review.

First of all, we're going to look at integrating multiple sources into a paragraph.

We start off with the topic sentence,e where you are outlining your main point or claim for the paragraph that you are writing. Think of this as your introduction to a small subsection of the literature review. You are giving an overview or high-level look at the topic. Next, you will bring in the supporting evidence to back up your initial statement or claim. However, it is worth noting that it is essential (and common) to find more than one source that supports your statements.

Additionally, we then follow this with some contrasting theories from other research. This is important as you need to show that you are considering a broad range of literature and not just cherry picking the bits that support your position!

Finally, you will typically conclude with a sentence that summarises your main points and think links to the next paragraph.

As we have previously discussed,d it is important to focus on the strengths and weaknesses of the work rather than simply stating what something is like. When describing the relevant work, it can be tempting to explain what a theory or principle says, however, it shows a greater level of understanding if you can explain why something is relevant or suitable for your work.

It is not simply enough to identify the different components of a piece of research, instead, you should focus on why the components are important and how they relate to each other. This applies to the different options that you might draw from your research – rather than simply stating what the options are you should provide reasoning and evidence for why you have chosen each of the options.

When describing literature, you would typically just list the key details of a piece of research; however, when critically appraising the work then you evaluate the significance of each option. These options may be listed in any order for a more descriptive piece of writing, whereas you should endeavour to structure the information in a logical order, whether that is importance, timeliness or thematic development.

As we have previously seen, being able to draw links between different sources and elements of research is important but highlighting why the links are there and what they mean.

The key thing to remember here is that you are aiming to draw conclusions based on the literature, rather than simply providing your reader with information!

# Critical Thinking

### As we have discussed so far critical thinking is vital when considering your literature review and so here are six questions that you can ask yourself when you discover a new piece of information or a new source.

### Who?

First of all you should consider who? This might be who has written the source in the first instance, who could benefit from it or who could stand to suffer as a result?

## What?

Next, you should consider the ‘what?’, which could refer to simply what are the strengths and weaknesses of the research, what is it trying to illustrate, or what can we do to make a positive change?

## Where?

The next question to ask yourself is ‘where?’. This could refer to where the research or information was published or made available but you might also consider where you might see it applied in the real world or even where you might go to get more information?

## When

It is important to consider when of any of your resources. This might be considering the temporal aspects for example, when it was published or created, or you might consider when you might expect something to change?

## Why?

‘Why?’ allows you to think about some of the broader elements of the information, such as why this is a problem or challenge in the first place, or why this is relevant today.

## How?

Finally, ‘how?’ considers how things are impacted or changed by this or how you might apply the informatio0n that you have just found.

# **What Makes a Good Literature Review?**

To finish off this LectureCast we will look at how you can evaluate your literature review, and break that down into the things that can constitute good and bad practice.

## Good Practices

### **Synthesis of available research and knowledge.**

One of the integral parts of a good literature review is the ability to take the available research and knowledge to create something new.

### **A critical evaluation.**

We have discussed the importance of critical thinking and some tricks to help you out, so applying these critical skills can be a really useful way to develop good practice.

### **Appropriate breadth and depth.**

You should aim to consider a broad range of subjects and areas at a good depth in order to fully appreciate a subject. For example, if you are developing a project looking at the authentication methods, then you might want to consider literature focusing on how humans interact with these systems. You would not want to limit yourself to purely technical literature.

### **Clear and concise**

The ability to write clearly and concisely is a universally useful skill and as such one that is especially relevant here.

### Tell a Story

Your literature review should paint a picture of why you are tackling a particular problem and how your work will sit alongside that. The aim is to develop a compelling narrative for your reader so that they can follow your logic and the story you are trying to sell.

### **Limitations**

Finally, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of your work. We have already discussed the importance of analysing the strengths and weaknesses of research and how nearly every piece of work can be improved or modified. Your literature review is no different; there will always be limitation,s and it is very important, and powerful, to understand these limitations and acknowledge them.

## Bad Practice

### **Annotated bibliography.**

There is often a temptation to simply perform a cursory overview of the literature, which amounts to nothing more than a bibliography with some of your own thoughts added to it.

### Confined to description.

We spent some time discussing the differences between description and critical thinking. In order to strive for best practice, it is important to avoid description where possible. Think critically!

### Narrow and Shallow

In contrast to what we said about the importance of breadth and depth in a literature review, a surface-level tour through a narrow range of subjects will not provide the foundations required for a good project.

### **References for the sake of references.**

It is quite common for literature reviews to become a simple exercise in collecting references. There is no minimum, or indeed maximum, number of references that you should use in your work, but it is very obvious when that work has been padded with references for the sake of it. You should aim to find the appropriate literature and consider it fully, highlighting why it is relevant and why it is included.

### **Not apparent how some sources relate to RQ(s).**

At this point in your project, you should have a good handle on your research questions and as such this can be used to drive the development of your literature review. It allows you to focus on the topics that support your research questions, providing a focused and structured piece of work.

### **Only covers research that validates assumptions.**

Finally, it is important to consider a wide range of research that supports various viewpoints. It will not benefit your project to merely cherry pick the sources that support your initial ideas, as this shows a lack of objectivity.

# Ethics

In this lecturecast, we are going to talk about research ethics and why they play an important role in your dissertation. However, before we dive into what you need to consider with regard to ethics and why that is important, we will take a step back to provide a definition of ethics.

Ethics can be thought of as the philosophical study of the concepts of moral right and wrong, it is the study of what is good and what is bad. You can also think of ethics as a set of moral principles that govern a person’s behaviour, or how we might conduct on an activity.

# Research Ethics

When we discuss ethics in the context of your project, we are focusing on research ethics, which are the moral principles that govern how you, as a researcher, should carry out your work.

Research ethics provide a set of standards of behaviour in relation to the rights of study participants and can be vital when planning, conducting, and reporting your research.

A university has an ethics board or group who are responsible for ensuring that any research activities are properly managed and carried out. Research ethics provides principles that are used to shape research regulations, which should be followed by all researchers.

# Importance of Ethics

Research ethics are important for your research for a number of reasons:

### Underline the Aims

In the first instance, they can help to promote the aims of your research, for example, expanding knowledge in a particular subject.

### Support Values

When carrying out research, it is important to foster the values that promote collaborative work, for example, mutual respect and fairness. This is important as scientific research is driven by the collaboration between scientists and research participants.

### Accountability

They mean that a researcher is held accountable for the work that they do and its implications. It is not uncommon for researchers to be supported by public money and so research ethics regulations help to manage and provide accountability on things like conflicts of interest, misconduct, research with human participants or how their data might be managed.

### Trustworthiness

Having these regulations in place can ensure that there is trust in research, and that it is appropriately managed.

### Promote Social and Moral Values

Finally, research ethics regulations can be used to ensure important social and moral values, for instance, things like doing no harm to others.

---

Ethics helps to promote integrity, objectivity, and honesty within research.

It provides a code of practice and a set of guidelines to ensure that research is carried out fairly and demonstrates the appropriate level of respect between the researcher and the participants.

However, research ethics are not in place to provide extra obstacles to your work; if anything, they promote a culture of academic freedom that enables really exciting and interesting research. It is your job as a researcher to show that you have fully considered the ethical processes in the design of your research, and most importantly, you obtain ethical approval for your work.

# **The Menlo Report**

The Menlo Report is an ethical framework that provides a set of basic principles to guide the identification and resolution of ethical problems relating to information and communication technology.

In reality, this extends to networks, hardware, and software, which involve technology relating to individuals or organisations. As we have previously discussed, our lives are becoming increasingly entwined with technology, whether that is monitoring our health data or performing day-to-day communications.

The Menlo report aims to create guidance for managing research in our information-centric society. The core principles are:

### Respect for Persons

This means ensuring research participation is voluntary and only occurs after getting informed consent. It is important to treat individuals as autonomous agents and respect their right to determine their own best interests. There should also be a consideration for the individuals who are not the target of the research but could still be impacted.

### Beneficence

This means that you should do no harm as a result of your research. Where possible, you should be looking to maximise the benefits and minimise any negative connotations associated with the work. This can be achieved through the use of risk assessments or similar.

### Justice

Refers to how you treat each individual person, and any benefits derived from the work should be evenly distributed according to individual need, contribution, and merit. The selection of your participants should be fair and transparent.

### Respect for the Law and Public Interest

Requires the researcher to be accountable for the research and to perform the requisite checks and investigations to ensure the legality of the research.

# Ethical Considerations

Based on the Menlo report, there is a range of things that you will need to consider when thinking about research ethics, which we will cover in greater depth. They are:

## Informed Consent

One of the most important elements of any research project is that you must get informed consent from your participants. This means that you must ensure that anyone who takes part in your research has a full understanding of the study. This includes giving a full description of the study and what they will need to do as part of that research.

If you are going to collect data from your participants, then you need to be clear about what you are going to collect and what it will be used for.

You should collect the consent from your participants in writing wherever possible, or if you are doing things digitally, for example, collecting data online, then you might wish to create a simple web form with a checkbox at the bottom that can be clicked once the participant has read and understood the terms and conditions.

There are some special cases for this, such as working with under 18s, however, as a good rule of thumb, it is best not to collect data from under 16s unless you have a very good reason and have thought about it very clearly.

## Deception

Sometimes it can be necessary for your participants not to be fully aware of some aspects of your research prior to taking part. For example, if we consider the Turing test, which says a participant should not be able to tell if they are conversing with a bot or a human in order to pass the test. To perform this experiment effectively then you would need to keep the information about who the participants are actually talking to a secret until after they had finished. This in itself is a form of deception, you are not giving all of the information to the people taking part in your study in advance.

This is a really simple example of deception, but there are a lot of things that you can do in this area, particularly in research relating to human behaviours, that can pose a greater risk of harm.

Therefore, it is really important to consider if you actually need to use deception to carry out your research or whether there is a less risky approach that you could take.

If you decide that it is important, then you will need to consider what the possible side effects and impacts on the participants could be, for example, are you likely to put them at an increased risk of harm by doing this?

Even if you do not provide them with a full description of the study in advance then you will need to fully debrief them at the end of the study. When deception is being used, there is usually a requirement to get informed consent for what your participant thinks they will be doing, and then debrief them afterwards to get consent for what they actually did. Once your participants are given the full details about the real purposes of the research then they need to have the right to withdraw.

## Freedom of Participation

When you are recruiting people to take part in your research then you must ensure that they are volunteers.

There should be no coercion in order to ensure the data you collect is ethically obtained. You should even avoid trying to get your friends to participate by agreeing to do their research, it should be entirely of their own free will!

There are times when you might incentivise your research to reimburse participants for the time they have given over to your research. It should never be perceived as a bribe or inducement to take part.

## Confidentiality

Whether you are collecting personal information about a group of people or whether you are observing the way individuals use a computer, you need to ensure that the data you collect is kept confidential.

This does not mean that you can't collect data, but if you do you then you should make sure your participants are not personally identifiable based on the data collected. For example, you might want to know about how a person's age impacts their social media usage. It's perfectly acceptable to keep the age bracket someone falls into, but not their specific date of birth.

Where possible, you should look to anonymise the data, potentially using participant numbers instead of anything that is easily identifiable, for example, name, address, or age. However, it is not one single piece of data that should be avoided. Data can often be aggregated to build a more complete picture of a user. For instance, if you collected age, occupation, education level, sexuality, and current location, then with each piece of data, you narrow down the possible people that could refer to!

Finally, you should consider the implications of data protection legislation, like GDPR, when you are designing your research and the way in which you will store data. You should only be collecting the data that you have specified in advance, and you should only use it for the agreed-upon purposes.

## Protection from Harm

As a researcher, you have an obligation to ensure that anyone who may take part in your study is not placed at risk of harm. While this can be a slightly loose definition, the general rule of thumb is to consider if taking part in the study places them at risk over and above everyday life.

**This can take the form of:**

1. Physical
2. Psychological
3. Emotional
4. Social
5. Spiritual
6. Reputation
7. Financial

## Observation

Research often requires you to observe the behaviours or actions of others, particularly when considering the way users might interact with technology. No matter what you are observing, you should always aim to get permission in the first instance.

For example, even using social media posts from a public account should be done so carefully. While the user may have made the information publicly available, that does not mean that they would be comfortable with the information being used for research purposes.

You should always look to gain permission when you are observing someone, whether they are public or private behaviours. Although as we have previously discussed, there may be elements of deception required in order to properly observe participants acting as naturally as possible.

## Debriefing

A debriefing will normally go hand-in-hand with any research that requires deception. A debriefing takes place after the participant has completed their part in the research and is where you will reveal the true purpose of the study, the data you have collected,d and how you intend to use that data.

This should be done before the data is pooled into a repository or store to enable the user to withdraw if they wish to. It might be necessary to provide a written summary of the findings as part of the debrief, dependent on the research you are carrying out.

You should ensure that there is a way for a participant to contact you should they have any concerns or wish to withdraw at a later date.

## Right to Withdraw

One of the most important requirements of any research study, which we have already touched on, is the need for participants to be able to withdraw from the research at any point.

However, this obviously comes with some caveats in that if the data has been aggregated and anonymised, it could be difficult for an individual to withdraw.

In order to allow participants to withdraw, you should give full instructions on the process and any necessary information when they complete the informed consent process.

When you are designing your research, it is important to fully consider the ethical issues as they can have far-reaching impacts on both you and your participants. While you might be able to produce something that is potentially interesting or exciting, it should never be at the expense of good ethical considerations and decision-making. Failure to consider the ethical implications can seriously impact the integrity of your research.

With that in mind, it is important that you are prepared to make pragmatic decisions about compromises to your proposed methodology to satisfy any ethical concerns. Your research can have a significant impact on you and your participants, and so you need to fully consider the potential of these outcomes.

# Ethical Expectations

When you are developing your project, there are a number of expectations on you as the researcher when considering how to act ethically.

### **Plan your research with ethical considerations in mind.**

When you plan your research, you should consider the ethical issues and prioritise the protection of your participants. This will allow you to design your research accordingly. You should always obtain University approval for your project, even if you are contributing to a study that has already achieved ethical approval.

### **Carry out and write up your research ethically**

Ensure that you act ethically when you are conducting your research and the way in which you report your research.

### Document the ethical considerations that you have made

You should document the ethical considerations clearly and concisely, ideally in a methodology chapter, although this will depend on the individual project. When you are documenting your considerations then you should think about how you factored ethics into the design of your research.

### **Document how you obtained ethical approval**

It is also useful to document how you obtained ethical approval in the first instance as well as demonstrating how you acted ethically in the collection and analysis of your data.

# Planning Your Objectives

## Research Plan Basics

**A dissertation is an extensive project that requires you to research a topic of your choice to produce a research artefact and your written report.**

In addition to this, one of the key components of a dissertation is managing your time, planning your project and ensuring you complete all of the requisite tasks.

In this unit, we are going to discuss the key elements of project planning, including an overview of the 2 most common project management methodologies used in computing or software-based projects, how you can manage your workload and tasks, as well as looking at the process of identifying and mitigating the potential risks that your project might face.

It is useful to work out how many weeks you have until you need to submit your completed dissertation and draw a chart showing these weeks.

Block out the weeks when you know you will be unable to work, and mark in other main commitments you have that will take time during this period. Then allocate research tasks to the remaining time.

One of the most common problems when project planning is being overly optimistic about the time it will take to perform a given task, it is much better to be realistic about the timeline than to set yourself unreasonable deadlines!

Some focused thought at the beginning, then at the planning stage of each phase, could save hours later on. Write down the resources needed for each stage. It could be time in the library, the resource of your working hours or the use of equipment or room space that needs to be booked in advance.

## Procrastination

Something that is very important to consider when planning your project is the very real risk of procrastination.

This is a very common problem and so there’s no point in trying to pretend it won’t happen or that it doesn’t exist. The best strategy is to acknowledge the inevitability of procrastination so that you can be well-prepared to deal with it when does start to happen.

**There are lots of reasons you might start to procrastinate, such as:**

1. **Time management issues where the project has not been properly planned in the early stages.**
2. **The tasks itself can feel overwhelming and just too big to tackle, which counterintuitively, is a key reason people avoid the work in hand.**
3. **Starting a dissertation is a significant task and often students can lack confidence in their ability to complete it successfully.**
4. **Everyone wants to do a good job of their dissertation, they want to reflect their skills, knowledge and understanding and so it is easy to strive for perfection**
5. **It is quite common to think that you work better under pressure as it can give you a real incentive to work harder.**

If you can spot the signs of procrastination early then you have a chance of minimising the knock-on impact on your project. It is useful to review what you expect to achieve and how realistic that actually is to try and get on top of any procrastination, this is where planning can be really helpful to guide you through the project.

## Be Realistic

To ensure that you are taking an achievable route to deliver your project, and to counteract the temptation to procrastinate, you need to consider:

- When can you realistically start work on the project? It is all too easy to assume that you will start work immediately and work as fast as you possibly can. In reality, this is unlikely to happen as you need time to understand the project, the research area, and to adjust to the requirements of a dissertation!
- There is a temptation to think that planning is a waste of time that could be better spent doing actual work. However, planning is absolutely essential in ensuring you follow the correct path, rather than being led down several dark alleys along the way. Think about driving somewhere that you have never been to before. If you just start without looking at a map, you will invariably get lost along the way. However, if you take some time upfront to work out the route, then you will ensure a straightforward and simple route.
- Are there some elements of your work that will take a specific amount of time to complete that you know about in advance, for example, applying for ethical approval.
- It is impossible to work on your project 24 hours a day, in fact it is impossible to get a solid 8 hours work out of a 9 to 5 day. There will always be other priorities that you cannot just ignore for the purposes of the project.
- The feeling that you have achieved something should not be underestimated, and so ensuring that you have a weekly goal that you can complete and maybe even set a reward for yourself for completing your objective for the week. This gives a feeling of progress and helps drive the project forward.

## Work Packages

At this point, you have already defined your research area and research problem and have finalised a research question that you are aiming to answer. This still leaves you with a fairly large piece of work to undertake. If you take the project as a single entity that you need to tackle all in one go then it will quickly seem unmanageable and daunting. This is where the idea of work packages comes in.

[](https://codahosted.io/docs/FCCNpRrEyp/blobs/bl-bz4ejzI4Kp/6a1fdce52e7dfbbcaad73136f6a4fa10633d829a0760ef56ecace2f0d4f496ecff58ed171f455d1e16d4d37d8fdb9655e4c0dbc623f6ec22dae8945a3085257eab8928d4d64e3cd50d4da90d671aa264fe45002026de60d6913d83409bb0a133bc184b37)

A work package is simply a way of breaking down a project into smaller more manageable chunks. You should have already considered the research objectives, the things that you need to achieve to support answering your research question, and work packages that will support the delivery of objectives to a degree. There is no hard and fast rule for subdividing your project into work packages, but having them associated with objectives and deliverables is a good starting point.

A simple way to think about a work package is a collection of related tasks that form a manageable chunk of a project. It’s a smaller set of steps within a larger, overarching project.

To give a real-world example, let’s imagine for a moment that you are in charge of building a new house. There are all sorts of different components and jobs that go towards building a house. We have plumbing, electrical, plastering, roofing, brick laying and many more. The point is that there lots of different things that need to be done before you have your finished house and each one of them is vital in supporting the delivery of your house.

One option would be to just order all of the materials that you need, recruit some helpers, and tell them to go and build a house. If you have the people with the right skills in your team, then it is entirely possible your house will get built in the end, but it is unlikely to be a problem-free project, and it is almost certainly not going to be built quickly. If you were to take this approach, imagine how difficult it would be to get an idea of how close you were to finishing the project!

However, if you break the process down into a number of smaller tasks, such as lay foundations, build the walls, fit the roof, and install the plumbing, then you have the makings of a project plan and your work packages. This has the added benefit of giving you a much better idea about the progress of the project, as you get feedback on each individual work plan rather than the project as a whole.

While building a house might seem a long way from your project, there are definitely similarities and it is equally helpful to divide your work into manageable chunks. Being able to identify the common goals and tasks associated with the elements of your project will enable you to plan things effectively and enable you to track your progress throughout the course of the project.

It is a good idea, although not strictly essential, to provide a deliverable associated with each work package. This has a number of benefits, including:

- Delivering a steady stream of progress as you go.
- Allowing early identification of any issues with the data, your methods or software.
- Giving a clear idea of the steps required to deliver the project.
- Providing transparency for you and your supervisor.

Let’s take a look at an example computing-centric project and the work packages that potentially be associated with it.

For this example, let’s imagine that we are working on a project that aims to collect and understand social media usage versus the desire for data privacy. As part of this project, our research question could be along the lines of ‘Does an individual’s social media usage determine their perceptions of privacy?’.

Our approach to this research is to develop and deploy a questionnaire to capture an individual’s social media presence and usage, and also their own personal feelings on the privacy of their data. This means that there are going to be a number of key elements of this project:

- Firstly, we will focus on understanding the current state of the art and the research field.
- Secondly, we will develop our questionnaire, which will also incorporate things like getting ethical approval.
- Next, we will collect the relevant data using a variety of means of distribution.
- The next stage will be to analyse the data using appropriate means.
- And finally, document your findings in your written report.

This broadly translates into five work packages that cover all of these key elements. It is important to note that there are a number of deliverables associated with some work packages. You are in no way limited to one deliverable per work package, and often work packages have several interrelated deliverables. For example, in work package 2, we are aiming to deliver a questionnaire and get ethical approval. It is impossible to get proper ethical approval without having a copy of the questions you will be asking, and so these two are closely related.

## Project Planning Approaches

Broadly speaking there are 2 generic approaches that you can take to deliver a project:

1. Plan-Driven
2. Agile

For the sake of your project, it will be tough to rigidly stick to an approach as you will largely be working alone, however, it is still worth considering the ethos you will try to adhere to and some of the key decisions you might want to make in terms of your approach to the work.

**We will give a quick overview of each of the approaches.**

### Plan-Driven:

A plan-driven approach is what we would view as a very traditional approach to designing and delivering a project. You create a plan for the work and you deliver that plan. Martin Fowler, one of the software engineers responsible for the inception of Agile development, described it as ‘you plan your work and then you work your plan’.

### Agile

Agile is focused on breaking your work down into small manageable chunks that deliver key functionality early and often. It’s a more reactive and less long-term approach to planning and management.

## Plan-Driven

This is the waterfall model and is typically what people mean when they talk about a plan-driven approach.

We are not going to go into great depth on the different types of plan-driven approaches or the relative strengths at this point. Instead, we will just discuss an overview of the approaches so that you can consider what works best for you and your project.

While this type of approach might be considered slightly out of date by many in the tech industry, it is still incredibly commonplace in large organisations or for large projects where oversight is key.

Here you divide your work into five key stages:

1. **Requirements Definition:** First you will define the requirements of your project, so at this point we are identifying what the project should do – effectively this will be the stage where you define your research question and objectives.
2. **Research Design**: The next stage is to design your research, one of the key features of the plan-driven approach to project management is that you can only move on to the next stage of the model once you have completed the current stage. In this instance this will mean that the design of your project is not carried out until you have properly specified and documented the requirements.
3. **Implementation:** Next is the implementation phase, which will typically be when you build your software artefact. If you are going to follow a plan-driven approach at this point,t you will look to develop exactly what you have specified in the design phase.
4. **Validation and Testing**: Validation and testing is where you will begin to analyse your data, identify correlations, and attempt to answer your research questions.
5. **Evaluation and Conclusions**: Finally, you will reflect on your project, draw conclusions, and consider future work.

Using an extensive project management methodology is probably going to be overkill given you are working on the project alone. However, it is certainly worth considering which structured approach you are going to use.

A plan-driven approach, such as a waterfall style approach, does offer some benefits.

You will produce some documentation to record the results of each stage, so for example, you will produce a list of requirements such as aim, objectives and research question at the end of your requirements gathering phase, or your work packages and research methodology at the end of the design phase.

It is normal in this kind of approach to try and deliver each phase of the project in sequence, although the reality is that they often overlap and feed into each other.

### **Why Use a Plan-Driven Approach?**

1. The first is that if you have a particularly well-defined and understood project then you might have a good idea about what you need to deliver and how you will go about it. If you are likely to have a project with static requirements then this could be a good approach.
2. It might be that you have previously worked on a project using this approach then it might be this is your preferred means of working and you have a significant amount of working in this way.
3. There is a certain amount of control with a method like this, in that you have clearly defined stages of your work with very clear criteria to move onto the next stage of the project. However, while this can be beneficial in some instances, if you have a project with shifting requirements or an uncertain outcome this will prove very difficult to manage.
4. Finally, there is a very clear plan from beginning to end with this approach and so it can make it easier to understand the workload and its distribution, however, the same caveat applies in that if you have a project with shifting scope then it can become difficult to manage.

## Agile

In contrast to a traditional plan-driven approach, there is a more responsive Agile approach to managing your project.

At its heart Agile development or project management is all about delivering key functionality early and often, so you are essentially highlighting the most important elements of your project and delivering these parts as soon as possible.

To give an example, it is all about focusing on the most important pieces of a solution first, so if we go back to building a house, we might aim to get the walls and roof built first to try and keep things water and weatherproof. Then, as time allows, you start to add the nice-to-haves.

This short term focus and planning gives you the ability to quickly respond to change. In the case of your project, you might discover new research that is relevant to your project and so an Agile approach would allow you to refocus

There are arguments that developing your project in this way allows you to focus on delivering a quality product, as you can really focus on producing something that meets your requirements and the needs of the project.

Agile development is governed by the Agile Manifesto, a set of 4 aims that underline its core messages.

1. **Individuals and Interactions over Processes and tools:** this means that understanding the problem and engaging with the right people is more important than the language you might use or the technology stack that you choose.
2. **Working over software over comprehensive documentation**: Producing something that actually works and meets your requirements is preferable to superfluous documentation, and while this is generally a good guiding principle, it is worth noting that the documentation in your project is very important and definitely cannot be ignored.
3. **Customer collaboration over contract negotiation:** While customer collaboration and contracts are not essential to your project, the next point certainly has value. Here we are interested in placing the emphasis on collaboration and communication, which is most likely to be with your supervisor.
4. **Responding to change over following a plan:** One of the greatest strengths of an Agile approach is that you are able to modify your plan based on any changes or new information, and in a research project, where the landscape can constantly shift, this is really important.

While it might usually be associated with development teams, there is an increasing number of Agile methods aimed at single developers, for example, Scrum Solo, although there is a number out there that are potentially worth investigating.

## What should you pick?

The big question is which method should you choose?

The answer is, sadly, it really depends! The decision is going to be based on a number of factors, including:

- The project.
- Your own experiences.
- Your personal preferences.
- Your own way of working. For example, do you like to do the majority of your planning up front or are you more comfortable planning as you go?

It is worth noting at this point that Agile is not just an excuse to avoid planning and make things up as you go! If you don’t put some thought into the direction of your project and how you are going to deliver it then it is unlikely to be as successful as you would like.

# Managing Your Workload

## Gantt Charts

Regardless of the method that you choose to plan and manage your project, it is still going to be vital to have a method documenting your plan and monitoring your progress. We are going to quickly look at 2 methods of planning and monitoring your project, which you may have already come across.

First, we will cover the more traditional Gantt chart, which is typically more suited to a plan-driven approach, as you will normally break your project down into a number of sequential work packages.

A Gantt chart is a project management tool assisting in the planning and scheduling of projects of all sizes, although they are particularly useful for simplifying complex projects. — **Association for Project Management (APM)**

A Gantt chart converts a project into a horizontal bar chart, where each line represents a particular work package or element of the project. The length of the bar is determined by the estimated time to complete the task, with the bar beginning and ending at the appropriate month, week, or day.

Essentially this is a visual simplification of your project showing which elements can be carried out in parallel, which also provides an overview of how long you think each element of your project should take to complete. In addition to this, you can also model dependencies between one or more work packages using a Gantt chart.

## Why use a Gantt Chart?

There are a number of reasons why you might want to use this approach to plan your project.

1. This approach provides a simple and fairly intuitive approach to displaying your project, its key elements and the timelines associated with them.
2. It will help you to define an initial schedule for the delivery of the different stages of your project.
3. Once this schedule is created, it will be much easier to modify and adjust the plan as needed.
4. The Gantt chart will provide you with an overview of your progress on the project, giving an at a glance view of where you should be at any given moment in time.
5. This will also help you to identify the key dates and deliverables in your project plan.
6. Finally, this will provide a means of communicating your plan and sharing your progress with your supervisor, for example.

### Potentially Pitfalls

It is important to note that there is some potential weaknesses inherent in using a Gantt chart:

- Firstly, it can become very easy to overcomplicate your project by breaking it down into too many different activities with a complex web of dependencies and relationships between them all. It is better to keep things simple and provide an easy-to-follow view of your project.
- A Gantt chart does not really provide any means of prioritising work or highlighting the tasks that are the most important.

## Using a Board

The alternative to a Gantt chart is to use a board, such as a Kanban, Jira, or other tracking board.

These boards are used to monitor the progress of a particular project by listing the issues or tasks that are associated with your project. These boards will typically allow you to manage, prioritise and monitor the progress of individual tasks as they are completed.

While there are a number of different boards that you can use to track your progress two of the most common are:

1. **SCRUM**: A Scrum board is targeted at projects using a sprint-based approach to deliver the work. This means that you will create a set of tasks to be completed in your sprint period (usually 1-2 weeks), which are added to the board. Then as you pick out and work on tasks they move through the different stages of your board, which might include things like in the queue, in progress, testing, completed.
2. **Kanban**: A Kanban board is aimed at continuous delivery rather than an iteration or sprint-based approach. You are aiming to create a list of all of the tasks that are required, and are constantly working on something.

### Why use a Board?

- **Flexibility:** First of all, it offers the flexibility that you would expect with an Agile approach, for example, if you suddenly find new research or uncover a new requirement then you can modify your tasks and priorities with very little effort.
- **Constant Delivery:** This approach is geared up to deliver functionality constantly, so that each task delivers something new and vital to your project.
- **Easy to Understand:** This approach is often very intuitive and easy to understand.

# Risks and Your Project

Risk can take many forms when considering your project. For example, this could be the risks to you as a researcher or the risks to your potential participants (although this will be covered with your ethical approval).

In this instance, we are going to discuss the things that could go wrong to prevent you from delivering your project successfully, and the mitigations that you can put in place to prevent them.

It is important to consider the risks that might impact your work as it is only by having an idea of the potential obstacles that you can properly plan your project.

The first and most important thing is to consider the risks that your project could face. Some of them are going to be fairly universal across all projects, whereas some are going to relate directly to your research topic and chosen methodology.

For example:

- One of the most common risks associated with the project is scope creep, which is where the work you are trying to complete rapidly expands and soon becomes unmanageable.
- If you are carrying out a project that requires data, whether that’s experimental or participant data, then it is very common to struggle to collect enough data to make reasoned conclusions and choices.
- Data loss, whether it is through losing physical media like a USB stick, drive or even a laptop or whether it is stolen or hacked, can have significant impacts to all involved. Whether this is the individuals whose data was stolen or the researcher in terms of loss of work or a punishment from the ICO.
- Finally, it is incredibly common for hardware to suddenly stop working at the most vital moments of a project, therefore, it is an ever-present risk when completing your projects.

## Mitigating Risks

Once we have identified the risks that our projects might face, it is important to think about we can go about preventing or, at the very least, limiting the impact of these risks.

**This is called a mitigation strategy.**

If we go back to our examples, then we could potentially:

- **Scope Creep - Have a Strong Plan in Place**: Ensure we have properly understood the requirements of our project, defined appropriate work packages and deliverables, and created a project plan, which enables us to prevent the risk of scope creep.
- **Unable to Collect Enough Data - Identify and Use Third-Party Datasets:** If there are concerns over the ability to collect a sufficient volume of data, then it is possible to potentially use existing third-party datasets from websites like Kaggle to demonstrate or validate your methods or software.
- **Data Lost or Stolen - Encrypt Data**: While it is impossible to ever mitigate the risk of human error, and so the risk of data being lost, we can manage the impact it can have by ensuring that all data is properly encrypted and so if it is lost it is far less damaging.
- **Computer Failure - Backup Your Work Remotely**: Computer hardware is fragile and so it is important to ensure you don’t have a single point of failure and so backing up your work to a remote service such as Github or Google Drive is a potentially useful strategy.

This is by no means an exhaustive list of risks or mitigations, however, it is important to consider the potential risks and think about how you can manage either their likelihood or impact when you start the project.

## Prioritizing Risks

Once you have identified the potential risks you can document the risks, their potential mitigations and risk scores in a risk register. This is a simple document that lets you keep a record of the potential risks that you have identified and how you will deal with them.

In order to assign some level of priority to each risk then it is normal to score them on at least two categories. The first in the likelihood of it happening, so what is the chance that this risk might occur and the second is the impact of that risk actually happening. There are a number of ways to score these two things, although for the sake of this example we are going to use a low, medium and high scoring system.

It is important to think about the risks as they relate to your project as this will allow you to properly plan to mitigate them.
